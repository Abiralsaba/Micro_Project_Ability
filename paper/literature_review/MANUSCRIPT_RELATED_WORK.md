## Accessible communication across heterogeneous interfaces

Remote assistive communication predates the present generation of edge IoT systems. PARLOMA connects hand-shape acquisition with networked robotic tactile reproduction, demonstrating a remote communication architecture for Deafblind interaction [P01](https://doi.org/10.5772/60416). Publish-subscribe assistance has also been used to connect visually impaired users with remote assistance providers [P02](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ifacol.2016.11.145). SymbolChat addresses remote picture-based communication with configurable interfaces for people with intellectual disabilities [P06](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2012.06.003). These systems differ in language, interaction and representation, but collectively show that remote communication across specialized access interfaces is not itself a new research contribution.

Other systems clarify the distinction between cross-modal conversion and remote protocol behavior. A comparison using the HaptiComm device reports that device-mediated tactile fingerspelling was harder to decipher than human-articulated fingerspelling, emphasizing a distinction between output production and intelligibility [P27](https://doi.org/10.1080/10400435.2024.2369547). A bidirectional Braille–speech prototype demonstrates a conversion architecture, although its evaluation used sighted/hearing participants and simulated impairment rather than Deafblind participants [P03](https://doi.org/10.7160/eriesj.2024.170206). MIO investigates local vibration-based communication with Deafblind users and exposes design needs around customization and playback [P05](https://doi.org/10.1109/HAPTICS52432.2022.9765570). AACrobat connects gaze-based AAC with a mobile companion to support autonomy and partner collaboration; its distributed architecture was studied primarily in co-located interaction [P04](https://doi.org/10.1145/2998181.2998215). Consequently, the existence of a network connection, a conversion path or a local usability study should not be treated as interchangeable evidence for reliable remote conversation.

Mixed-ability communication also involves requirements that cannot be reduced to endpoint compatibility. Research on ability-diverse collaboration examines how systems account for users' abilities and collaborative roles [P09](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3641930). Studies of virtual teams and collective access identify interactions between accessibility practices, preferences and organizational context [P10](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790419), [P12](https://doi.org/10.1145/3800645.3813075). Information transformations within mixed-visual-ability teams provide further evidence that adapting representations can involve human work and tradeoffs [P11](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790872). These findings motivate user control over presentation and fallback, while limiting claims that automatic modality selection alone resolves accessibility.

## Architectural and interoperability foundations

Modality-independent interaction has established architectural foundations. The W3C Multimodal Interaction Framework separates interpretation, interaction management, generation and presentation [S02](https://www.w3.org/TR/mmi-framework/). Multimodal Architecture and Interfaces subsequently specifies an interaction-manager/modality-component architecture with lifecycle events suitable for distributed implementations [S01](https://www.w3.org/TR/2012/REC-mmi-arch-20121025/). EMMA provides a representation for multimodal interpretation annotations [S03](https://www.w3.org/TR/emma/). Together, these specifications challenge novelty claims based solely on a shared intermediate representation, a hub that coordinates adapters or a generic modality lifecycle.

Capability and preference infrastructure likewise precedes AMP. Web of Things descriptions and discovery provide machine-facing mechanisms for describing and locating device services [S04](https://www.w3.org/TR/wot-thing-description11/), [S05](https://www.w3.org/TR/wot-discovery/). Universal remote-console and flexible-interface work addresses replaceable user interfaces, while personal-needs standards describe preferences for accessible digital delivery [S17](https://www.iso.org/standard/60821.html), [P15](https://doi.org/10.3233/AIS-160370), [S18](https://www.iso.org/standard/43603.html). Human-language negotiation over SIP addresses language choices per medium and direction [S14](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8373.html). These mechanisms serve different layers: a device's supported output is not equivalent to a user's preference or authorization to use it. An assistive protocol should specify their relationship rather than present each as a new primitive.

IoT middleware provides another substantial source of overlap. INTER-IoT's CasAware case integrates platforms with semantic mediation [P13](https://doi.org/10.3233/AIS-200578), and semantic middleware research explicitly addresses service discovery, selection and composition [P14](https://doi.org/10.1002/ett.4544). CLAID and a multilayer multimodal edge architecture illustrate distributed execution and platform support in related application settings [P17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2024.05.026), [P16](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24165199). Such work establishes that heterogeneity and edge deployment are comparison requirements. Their reuse in an assistive platform can be valuable without constituting a distinct communication mechanism.

## Delivery, presentation and receiver control

Reliable communication requires careful identification of the event being acknowledged. MQTT defines protocol delivery and session behavior [S06](https://docs.oasis-open.org/mqtt/mqtt/v5.0/os/mqtt-v5.0-os.html). XMPP message receipts report delivery to a recipient client, whereas displayed markers report a client display position [S19](https://xmpp.org/extensions/xep-0184.html), [S09](https://xmpp.org/extensions/attic/xep-0333-1.0.0.html). Output completion also has earlier multimodal precedents: EMBASSI's output modeling discusses acknowledgment after presentation, and MMI provides component lifecycle feedback [P26](https://cdn.aaai.org/Workshops/2002/WS-02-08/WS02-08-006.pdf), [S01](https://www.w3.org/TR/2012/REC-mmi-arch-20121025/). Presentation-aware acknowledgment therefore cannot be claimed as an AMP invention. Furthermore, completion of an actuator or audio operation is not evidence of human comprehension; effectiveness requires a separate observation or study outcome.

Receiver pacing is similarly established at multiple layers. TCP controls the byte stream through the receiver window, AMQP provides receiver link credit, and Reactive Streams specifies demand-driven backpressure [S15](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html), [S07](https://docs.oasis-open.org/amqp/core/v1.0/os/amqp-core-complete-v1.0-os.pdf), [S08](https://github.com/reactive-streams/reactive-streams-jvm/blob/v1.0.4/README.md). RFC 8865 is especially relevant to accessible conversation because it allows negotiation of the receiver's maximum text character rate [S13](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8865.html). The scientific question is therefore not whether a slow receiver should influence a fast sender. It is whether presentation-state information supports a demonstrably better control policy than a well-tuned implementation of established feedback mechanisms.

## Recovery at the assistive presentation boundary

Existing recovery mechanisms address several parts of interruption. XMPP supports stream resumption, the Bundle Protocol supports disruption-tolerant forwarding, and stateful dataflow research supplies computational checkpoint mechanisms [S10](https://xmpp.org/extensions/xep-0198.html), [S11](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9171.html), [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.08603). AMQP additionally describes transfer resumption using received message-section positions [S07](https://docs.oasis-open.org/amqp/core/v1.0/os/amqp-core-complete-v1.0-os.pdf). These are important precedents, but a transfer offset does not by itself identify how much tactile or spoken content a person has perceived. The distinction becomes consequential when physical rendering occurs before a crash but the corresponding durable progress record is unavailable.

This boundary motivates a narrower investigation than a general cross-disability protocol claim. Within the reviewed sources, evidence is limited for an evaluated contract that jointly reconciles partial assistive presentation, constrains fallback by user authorization and regulates remaining presentation work after interruption. This observation is scoped to a nonexhaustive review and does not establish that an equivalent solution is absent. AMP should therefore test whether a joint progress/recovery/admission mechanism improves reliability or communication outcomes over a composition of established lifecycle, persistence, checkpoint and receiver-credit mechanisms. If that composition provides equivalent guarantees and outcomes, AMP's contribution should be characterized as integration rather than protocol novelty.
